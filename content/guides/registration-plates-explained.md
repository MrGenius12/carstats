Every car registered in Ireland carries a plate built from three parts: a year identifier, a county code, and a sequence number. The format looks simple, but one detail catches out almost every buyer of an imported car. The plate is **not** based on the year the car arrived in Ireland, or even the year it was built. It is based on the date the car was **first registered anywhere in the world**.

This guide sets out how the format works, how the county codes are assigned, and how imported vehicles are plated, which is the part that causes most confusion.

## The format, before and after 2013

Two formats have been used since the modern system began in 1987.

| Period | Format | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 January 1987 to 31 December 2012 | `YY-CC-SSSSSS` | `11-D-12345` | Two-digit year only |
| Since 1 January 2013 | `YY[1/2]-CC-SSSSSS` | `131-D-12345` / `132-D-12345` | Third digit splits the year into two halves |

Since 2013, the year identifier carries an extra digit for the half of the year in which the vehicle was first registered: **1** for January to June, **2** for July to December. So a car first registered in the first half of 2026 shows **261**, and one from the second half shows **262**. The half-year split was introduced in 2013 partly to avoid a plain "13" on plates, and partly to spread new car sales more evenly across the year.

**CC** is the county code, covered below, and **SSSSSS** is a sequence number of up to six digits, issued in order within that county for that period.

## Imports: the part most buyers get wrong

The single most important rule on an Irish plate is this: **the year identifier reflects the date of first registration, wherever that took place.** It is not the date of import into Ireland, and it is not the vehicle's model year.

Revenue states this directly, with a worked example: a car imported into Ireland in 2017 but first registered in the UK in 2011 is plated **11-xx-xxxx**, not 17-xx-xxxx.

Applied to a more recent case, a 2016 UK-market car cleared through Irish registration in 2026 will be plated **161-D-xxxxx** or **162-D-xxxxx**, depending on which half of 2016 it was first registered in the UK, not 261 or 262. The plate tells you when the car first went on the road, not when it landed in Ireland.

The sequence number (**SSSSSS**) works differently. It is assigned in the order the car is actually processed through Irish registration, so an import registered in 2026 receives the next available number for its plate year in that county, alongside cars that were genuinely new in that year.

Before 2015, buyers could reserve a specific plate number using form VRT15A even for a used import. That option was withdrawn for used imports in 2015, and reservation is now available for new vehicle registrations only.

### What to look for

- Treat the year identifier on an imported car as its true first-registration date, not a guide to when it entered the Irish market.
- Cross-check the plate year against the logbook or import documentation rather than assuming it matches the purchase or import year.
- An older-looking plate on a recently imported car is normal and is not in itself a warning sign.

## County codes

The letter (or letters) in the middle of the plate identifies the local authority area where the vehicle was first registered in Ireland. For an import, that means the owner's address at the point of Irish registration, not where the car came from abroad.

| Code | County | Code | County |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | Cork | LM | Leitrim |
| CE | Clare | LS | Laois |
| CN | Cavan | MH | Meath |
| CW | Carlow | MN | Monaghan |
| D | Dublin | MO | Mayo |
| DL | Donegal | OY | Offaly |
| G | Galway | RN | Roscommon |
| KE | Kildare | SO | Sligo |
| KK | Kilkenny | T | Tipperary |
| KY | Kerry | W | Waterford |
| L | Limerick | WH | Westmeath |
| LD | Longford | WW | Wicklow |
| LH | Louth | WX | Wexford |

Most two-letter codes are simply the first and last letters of the county's English-language name: Sligo becomes **SO** and Clare becomes **CE**, for instance.

Limerick, Tipperary and Waterford each used two separate codes until the end of 2013: **L** and **LK** for Limerick City and County, **TN** and **TS** for the North and South Ridings of Tipperary, and **W** and **WD** for Waterford City and County. From 1 January 2014 each county has a single code (**L**, **T** and **W**). Older plates with the previous codes remain valid and are not reissued.

## FAQ

### Does an Irish plate show the car's manufacture year?

No. It shows the year (or half-year) the car was first registered. For new Irish-market cars this is usually the year it was built, but it can differ for used imports, personal imports, and cars that sat unregistered for a period.

### What year does an imported car get on its Irish plate?

The year it was first registered in its country of origin, not the year it was imported into or registered in Ireland. Revenue's own guidance confirms this with a worked example of a car imported in 2017 but plated 11-xx-xxxx because it was first registered in the UK in 2011.

### Can I choose or reserve a specific registration number for an imported car?

Not since 2015. Plate reservation via form VRT15A remains available for new vehicle registrations, but the option was withdrawn for used imports.

### Does the county code have to match where I live now?

No. It reflects the address used at the point of first Irish registration. It does not change if the owner later moves to a different county, and it stays with the vehicle for life, including when the car changes hands.
